# Our Heroine Trims the Tack

Last chapter ended with two real, working horses — the Swiss franc and the Japanese yen, both cleared to run, both improved since with a longer memory and a proper read on whether they were trending or chopping sideways. This chapter is about a much smaller, much more tempting idea: now that our heroine finally had a stable's worth of diagnostic tools, she pointed one of them — the permutation-importance chart, the one that shuffles a single feature and watches how much the model's performance falls apart without it — back at her own winning models, expecting to trim the fat. What she found instead was a lesson about the difference between a feature being *individually* useless and a feature being *safely removable*, and those turned out to be two very different claims.

**The Case for a Lighter Saddle**

The permutation-importance chart had been sitting there since the last time she'd run it, and it told a consistent, almost boring story across both winning horses: a handful of features — a multi-day trend read, a matching volatility read, a momentum indicator — did essentially all of the real work. Meanwhile, a whole cluster of other features sat at the bottom of the chart with an importance score indistinguishable from exactly zero: which trading session was open, which day of the week it was, the change in the spread, the change in volume, even a cross-currency "broad dollar strength" signal she'd built by hand earlier in the investigation. Zero, repeatedly, on both horses.

The temptation was obvious. If a piece of tack isn't doing anything, take it off. A model carrying nine columns of dead weight alongside the columns that actually matter is a model that's slower to train, harder to reason about, and — by the same logic that had already burned her once this investigation, when a redundant feature quietly destabilized a model that looked fine at first glance — a model with more surface area for something subtle to go wrong. Trimming down to just the load-bearing features looked like a strictly good idea, maybe even a free one.

**Designing an Honest Test, Not a Victory Lap**

She'd learned enough by this point not to just try the trimmed version once and declare success if the number went up. The real question wasn't "does the lean model look fine" — it was "does it hold up exactly as well, across every condition the full model had already been tested under." So she ran the comparison properly: both the plain symmetric version of each horse's race and the more aggressive "cut losses short, let winners run" version, on both winning horses, and — as a control group, so a lucky coincidence on just two animals couldn't fool her — on three horses that had already failed to qualify for anything. Twenty separate races in total, run the same rigorous way every race in this whole investigation had been run: five non-overlapping stretches of real history each, judged as a whole rather than one favorable slice.

**The Losers Stayed Losers, Which Was the Point**

The three already-disqualified horses were the easiest read: trimmed or not, symmetric or aggressive, all twelve of those races came back exactly as unqualified as before. Not one of them so much as flirted with passing. That was reassuring in its own right — it meant the trimming exercise wasn't secretly a way of squeezing noise into looking like signal, which is a real risk whenever you're comparing many versions of many models against each other. There was one small, consistent wrinkle worth noting honestly: the trimmed versions of these three losers were, every time, a little *less* bad than their full-featured counterparts — never remotely close to qualifying, but a small, repeatable nudge in the right direction. That's a plausible hint that some of that dead weight adds a little overfitting noise when there's genuinely nothing to find, which is a fair, modest point in favor of trimming — just not the point that mattered most.

**The Winners Didn't Feel the Same Way**

The two actual contenders told a different story, and not the one being hoped for. The Swiss franc's plain symmetric race, which had been cleanly qualifying on every single measure tried, lost two of its five confidence levels outright the moment the "useless" columns were removed — the model wasn't just a little worse, it stopped clearing the bar entirely at its two most cautious settings. The yen's plain symmetric race kept its nominal qualification, but the more demanding seed-by-seed stress test — the one that reruns the whole race under several different starting conditions to make sure the result isn't a fluke of one lucky draw — showed the same story underneath: reliability that had been holding at five, sometimes six, out of six different starting conditions dropped to as few as three. The one place trimming didn't clearly hurt was the aggressive "cut losses short" version of the franc's race, where it came out roughly a wash — which, on reflection, is the closest thing to a genuine partial win this experiment produced.

**Why "Useless Alone" Doesn't Mean "Useless"**

The honest explanation isn't complicated once you sit with it, and it's the actual moral of this chapter. The permutation-importance test asks one very specific question: if you scramble this one column and leave every other column exactly as it was, how much worse does the model get? That's a fair test of a feature's *marginal* contribution, holding everything else fixed — but it says nothing about what happens when you remove that feature from the model's field of view *permanently*, changing the whole landscape the model's decision trees get built from. A tree-based model builds itself one split at a time, and a wider menu of candidate columns to split on — even columns that don't look individually important — can still lead to a better sequence of splits than a narrower menu would have, in a way that a one-at-a-time shuffle test simply isn't built to detect. Individually replaceable and collectively removable are not the same claim, and this chapter is the receipt.

**Where This Leaves the Stable**

Nothing changed in either winning horse's actual configuration — the lean, trimmed versions are being left in the stable rather than raced, and the full-featured versions that were already winning keep their tack exactly as it was. What did change is a small but real addition to how this whole investigation now thinks about "simplify the model" as an instinct: it's a reasonable thing to *test*, the same way every other idea in this project has had to earn its place with a full, honest trial — but it is not a reasonable thing to assume, not even when the evidence for it looks as clean as a flat line at zero on an importance chart.
