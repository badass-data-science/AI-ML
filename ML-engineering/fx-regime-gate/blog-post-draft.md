# Our Heroine Builds a Traffic Light Out of Two Rejected Hypotheses

Our heroine had a list of five ideas sitting in a text file, the kind of list you write at 11pm after a good analysis session, when everything still seems tractable. Idea number five was the sort of thing that sounds great before you've touched any data: watch the currency-pair correlation network she'd built for structural bursts — moments where an unusual number of pairs suddenly flip their lead-lag relationships all at once — and use those bursts as a homemade, calendar-free early-warning system for market shocks. No economic calendar required. Just vibes, graph theory, and partial correlations. She was, frankly, a little in love with the idea before she'd tested it.

Then she tested it, and it did the thing good ideas do about forty percent of the time: it turned out to be half wrong, in an interesting way, and the interesting way turned into a small, useful tool instead of the big flashy one she'd originally imagined. This is the story of how a rejected hypothesis about the Federal Reserve and a partially-rejected hypothesis about market volatility got welded together into a two-signal dashboard that does one honest, narrow job.

**The Setup**

The tool in question is fx-pcn, her partial-correlation network project that rebuilds a graph of seven major currency pairs every week, using graphical lasso to figure out which pairs are structurally related right now and Granger causality to figure out which pair is leading which. Every week, some of the directed edges in that graph flip — a pair that was leading yesterday starts following today, or a relationship that didn't exist before shows up. Most weeks, a handful of pairs flip. Some weeks, a lot of them flip at once. Idea number five was: what if "a lot of them flip at once" is itself a signal — a structural earthquake detector that doesn't need a news calendar to notice something happened?

To test that, she needed two things: a definition of "burst," and something to check bursts against. The definition came for free from the data itself — across 606 weekly evaluation steps from 2015 to 2026 (a 60-day rolling correlation window, stepped forward 7 days at a time), the number of flips per week has a mean of about 3.4 and a right-skewed tail running up to 13. The extreme tail — 7 or more flips in a week, roughly the top 6% of all weeks — lined up suspiciously well with dates she already recognized without looking anything up: March 16th and 23rd, 2020 (the COVID crash), February 7th, 2022 (two and a half weeks before the invasion of Ukraine), January 2016 (the China market turmoil). That's a good sign for a detector. It's also not proof of anything — recognizing famous crisis dates in a list of high numbers is exactly the kind of thing a human brain does whether or not there's a real signal there, so the next step was to check it against something less anecdotal.

**Chapter One, In Which the Federal Reserve Is Innocent**

The first calendar-free-detector claim to test was the calendar itself: does the burst signal actually track scheduled FOMC statement and minutes releases? If bursts are just "the network noticing the Fed is about to move," that's useful information, but it isn't calendar-free at all — you could get the same thing by, well, checking a calendar.

The direct test said no. Comparing burst weeks to the nearest FOMC release date, the correlation was essentially zero (Spearman r = -0.05), and a permutation test on "how many flips happen near a Fed statement versus a random week" came back with p = 0.18 — not significant. High-burst weeks sat within three days of a Fed statement about as often as any other week did. The network, whatever it's doing, is not watching the FOMC calendar.

That would have been a clean, boring negative result, except she'd already run a separate experiment using Granger causality tests against the same FOMC calendar, in a notebook that hadn't been annotated yet — the kind of notebook every data scientist has, full of cells that ran once, produced a number, and never got explained to future-you. That notebook's headline number looked spectacular: an F-test on "does an FOMC statement Granger-cause next week's flip count" came back with a p-value small enough to need scientific notation with a minus-nine in the exponent. That's not a subtle effect. That's the kind of number that makes you want to write the paper immediately.

It's also, it turned out, complete nonsense — and finding out why is the more useful part of this story than the negative result itself. FOMC statement dates are scheduled months and years in advance by the Federal Reserve's own calendar. A currency-pair correlation network flipping edges cannot possibly cause a Fed meeting to happen. So as a sanity check, she ran the test backwards — does the network's flip count "Granger-cause" the Fed's pre-scheduled statement dates, a relationship that is logically impossible in reality — and that impossible direction came back *more* significant than the real one, at nearly a p-value of 10 to the minus twelve. When the impossible direction of a causality test looks just as strong (or stronger) than the plausible one, that's the standard tell that both variables are picking up some third, shared thing — in this case, the FOMC's rigid, near-periodic six-week meeting schedule — rather than one actually causing the other. She confirmed this wasn't a fluke by shuffling which days counted as "statement days" 200 times and rerunning the test: real FOMC dates blew way past anything a random shuffle produced, meaning the effect was real, just not causal — it was the schedule's *regularity*, not the events themselves, driving the number. Sweeping every FOMC variable against every network metric in that old notebook (21 combinations in total) showed the identical pattern everywhere. Case closed: the Fed is innocent, the notebook's exciting number was an artifact, and idea five's "calendar-free" claim survived — just not for the reason she'd hoped.

**Chapter Two, In Which VIX Is Guilty, But Only a Little**

If not the Fed, then what about volatility more broadly? The market's own fear gauge, the VIX, seemed like the more natural thing for a "structural shock detector" to be picking up. Same test, different suspect: does elevated VIX co-occur with burst weeks?

The honest answer is: barely, and only at the extreme. Across the whole distribution, flip count and VIX level are essentially uncorrelated (Spearman r = 0.055, not significant), and splitting VIX into quartiles shows a flat relationship across the bottom three — knowing VIX is merely "somewhat elevated" tells you almost nothing about how many pairs are about to flip. But the top burst weeks — the same extreme tail that lined up with COVID and the Ukraine buildup — do have a real, statistically solid difference: a median VIX of 22 during high-burst weeks versus 16 for everything else, a gap that survives even after throwing out the two COVID weeks entirely so they can't be doing all the work alone (Mann-Whitney p = 0.0035 with COVID excluded, p = 0.0007 with it included). So: elevated volatility looks necessary but not sufficient for a burst. Most high-VIX weeks pass without incident. But when the network does burst, it's reliably during a period the broader market already recognizes as stressed, not a calm one.

That raised the more interesting question, and the one that actually determined what kind of tool this became: which one moves first? A detector is only useful as an early warning if it moves *before* the thing it's warning about. So she ran a bidirectional Granger causality test between VIX and flip count, and — learning from Chapter One's mistake — tested it against a rigorous placebo this time: instead of comparing to a naive shuffle, she shifted the VIX series by a random number of weeks (a "circular shift"), which keeps VIX's own internal week-to-week behavior completely intact while destroying its real-world timing relationship to the flip count series. If the original result was just VIX's own autocorrelation fooling the test, a randomly-shifted VIX should do just as well. It didn't. The real, correctly-timed VIX series beat all 200 randomly-shifted versions, and the forward direction — VIX leading flip count by one to ten weeks, strongest at two weeks out — was statistically real (p = 0.0006 at the peak lag) while the reverse direction, flip count leading VIX, was flat as a pancake at every lag tested.

Put plainly: VIX moves first. The network's bursts show up *after* volatility is already elevated, not before. Idea five, as originally conceived — a novel early-warning system that beats the market to the punch — doesn't survive contact with the data. But a signal that reliably confirms, one to ten weeks after the fact, that elevated volatility has become structural in the currency market specifically, rather than staying contained to equities — that's still a real, useful, and now well-tested thing. It's just a different tool than the one she set out to build.

**The Gate**

So instead of an early-warning siren, she built a confirmation gate — a small two-signal dashboard that lives in this `fx-regime-gate` directory, alongside this post. It reads the same fx-pcn output used throughout the analysis (the 60-day-window, 7-day-step, daily network) and pulls a current VIX quote, then reports one of three colors:

**RED** means VIX is elevated (20 or above, close to the 22-median seen in real historical burst weeks) *and* the network has burst (7 or more flips) within the last two weekly steps. This is the one state the whole investigation actually validated: the two signals agreeing has survived every placebo test thrown at it. It says the market-wide stress everyone can already see in VIX is, specifically, showing up as structural change in FX correlations too — not staying contained to equities, credit, or wherever else VIX-driving stress usually starts.

**YELLOW** means only one signal is firing — VIX up without a recent burst, or a burst without VIX being particularly elevated. This is the common case, and on its own it means almost nothing; most elevated-VIX weeks never produce a burst at all, so a yellow reading is closer to background noise than a warning.

**GREEN** means neither signal is firing — a quiet week by both measures, which, as of this writing, is exactly where things stand: VIX at 15.2, and the last burst happened forty-five weeks ago.

The script itself is a single Python file, `gate.py`, that anyone can run with `python3 gate.py` as long as fx-pcn's own pipeline has already populated its output directory. It prints a plain-text report, including a "how to read this gate" section built directly into every run, so the caveats travel with the result instead of living only in a paper nobody rereads before making a decision. That section says, in slightly more measured language than this post: this is a lagging confirmation signal, not a predictive one, so don't expect it to move before VIX does; the underlying burst signal is a tail effect, not a general-purpose correlate, so don't over-read a single YELLOW; and the FOMC calendar was deliberately left out of this tool entirely, because — as established above — the Fed did not do it.

**Why This Is the Right Amount of Tool**

It would have been easy to keep chasing the original, more ambitious version of idea five — an early-warning shock detector, something that beats the news. The data said no to that fairly unambiguously, twice, in two different ways (the FOMC direction and the VIX-leads-not-lags direction), and both rejections came with the kind of methodological receipts — reverse-direction placebos, circular-shift placebos, shuffle tests — that make a negative result trustworthy rather than just discouraging. What was left after both rejections wasn't nothing, though: a real, tail-specific, placebo-surviving relationship between currency-market structural change and broad market fear, one that answers a genuinely different question than VIX alone can answer. VIX tells you the market is scared. The gate tells you whether that fear has become an FX-structural fact rather than staying an equity-desk problem — which matters quite a lot if the next question is whether to trust a currently-"validated" forex trading model, or go check on it first.

That's next on the list, and it isn't built yet: nobody has gone back and checked whether the gate's red periods actually would have flagged the moments when her forex-ML models started drifting out of validation. Until that backtest exists, this is a situational-awareness sketch, not a risk-management system — an instrument panel light, not an autopilot.
