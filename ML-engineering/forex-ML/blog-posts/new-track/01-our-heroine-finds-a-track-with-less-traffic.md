# Our Heroine Finds a Track With Less Traffic

Last time we left our heroine, she had just done the hardest thing a scientist ever has to do: close a case honestly. Two different kinds of model, two different questions asked of them, four separate rounds of richer information, and one closely-audited scoreboard, all pointing the same direction — there was no edge left to find on the biggest, most heavily-traded currency pair in the world, at least not with what was available to look at it with. She didn't call it a loss. She called the toolkit she'd built a stable, and promised the next chapter would start on a new track entirely, one with less traffic on it. This is that chapter, and it turns out "less traffic" was not just a nice turn of phrase.

**Casting a Wider Net**

The theory was simple enough to state and expensive enough to actually check: maybe the problem was never the method, it was the racetrack. EUR/USD is the single most-watched, most-arbitraged, most professionally-picked-over instrument in the entire foreign exchange market — if there was ever going to be a leftover scrap of signal cheap enough for one independent project to find, it was never going to be hiding in the most obvious place. So the search went wider. Gold first, since it isn't even technically a currency pair and trades on a different set of instincts than the majors. Then a small, deliberately chosen handful of currency crosses that don't route through the US dollar at all — pairs with genuinely less institutional traffic on them. Then, for completeness, the two remaining original majors that had never actually been tried.

Most of what came back was a clean, boring negative, and our heroine has learned to be genuinely grateful for boring negatives, because she has also learned exactly how expensive it is to mistake an exciting-looking one for real. One cross looked promising in a single test window and evaporated the moment it was checked against five separate ones instead of one — a straightforward case of a lucky stretch of history flattering a mediocre model, caught before anyone got attached to it. USD/CAD did something sneakier: it beat its baselines comfortably on raw accuracy while quietly losing money at every single confidence level, a shape that looks like "maybe more features would tip this into profit" right up until you actually validate it, at which point it turns out to be two wildly lucky test windows and two catastrophic ones canceling out to something that merely looks unlucky. Gold, of all things, was the one genuine surprise — a real, statistically defensible edge that survived every check thrown at it, undercut only by the thoroughly unglamorous fact that the trading account in question isn't actually allowed to trade gold. A validated edge with no track to run it on.

**The One That Actually Held Up**

Then came the Swiss franc against the US dollar, and it did something none of its cousins had managed: it beat both baselines on accuracy, and its net result, instead of a clean improving-with-confidence line or a clean negative, did something odd — negative at low confidence, then decisively positive in the middle range, then flat again at the very top. That kind of wobble usually means noise. But the diagnostic that had caught every previous false alarm — checking whether the test period just happened to look different from the training period — came back clean. Nothing here to explain away.

So it went through the full test: not one lucky stretch of history, but five separate, non-overlapping ones, stitched together and judged as a whole. And for the first time in this entire investigation, every single result came back positive. Every one of five confidence thresholds, across every one of five separate stretches of market history — twenty-five results, twenty-five wins for the strategy, not one exception. The win rate climbed cleanly as the model's own confidence rose, the statistical tests agreed with each other, and even after being properly penalized for testing five different thresholds at once rather than picking a favorite after the fact, every single one still cleared the bar. It is, by a comfortable margin, the strongest result this whole project has produced.

**Trusting It Anyway**

A model that only works because of the particular sequence of random numbers used to build it is not a model our heroine trusts, so she rebuilt this one six separate times, each with a different starting seed, and watched to see whether the result held up or just happened once. The good news: the sign never flipped, not once, across any seed or any of the five test stretches — this was never a coin that came up heads by luck. The more honest news: the *strength* of the result wasn't identical seed to seed. Four of the six seeds were comfortably, confidently significant. Two were real but noticeably weaker — the kind of number that would make a careful person hesitate before betting the farm on the exact figures from any single run. This is not the same failure mode that once forced an entire model architecture into retirement, where the whole thing would occasionally just give up and predict the same class every time. This is smaller, subtler, and arguably more honest: a real effect, whose exact size depends a little on luck of the draw, same as almost everything else worth measuring in a genuinely noisy market.

**An Experiment in Temperament**

Every trader eventually asks some version of the same question: should you cut your losses fast and let your winners run, or treat a win and a loss as symmetric events? So far, every result in this whole investigation had used the symmetric version — profit target and stop-loss set to the same distance. Loosening that assumption on the Swiss franc result produced something genuinely interesting rather than just a bigger number. Tightening the stop relative to the target flipped the win rate to something below a coin flip — the strategy now loses more often than it wins, on paper a worse-looking horse entirely. And yet the money made per trade went *up*, not down, because the wins that did land were bigger than the losses that didn't, comfortably enough to more than make up the difference, a pattern confirmed by the kind of statistical test built specifically for telling "wins more" apart from "wins bigger." This new, more aggressive temperament also turned out to be the more seed-stable of the two — every single one of six seeds cleared significance, not just four of six. Tightening the stop even further, on the theory that more of a good thing is more good, actually made things worse rather than better — proof that this is a real sweet spot to be found, not a dial that only goes one useful direction.

**Asking the Model What It's Actually Looking At**

None of the tree-based models used anywhere in this project come with a built-in explanation of their own reasoning the easy way, so getting one out requires asking a slightly ruder question directly: scramble one piece of information at a time and see how much the model's performance falls apart without it. Run across both configurations of the Swiss franc result, the answer was reassuringly boring in the best possible way. A single feature capturing roughly four days of trend utterly dominated everything else, followed closely by a matching read on longer-horizon volatility, followed by a classic momentum indicator. Everything describing which trading session was open, or which day of the week it happened to be, moved the needle by a number indistinguishable from exactly zero, in both configurations, across every single test stretch. The model isn't leaning on some brittle calendar coincidence — it's leaning on trend, volatility, and momentum, three ideas any trader from any decade would recognize as legitimate reasons to have an opinion about where a price is headed. The most recent handful of trading days mattered most, unsurprisingly, but a meaningful amount of signal was still coming from clear on the other side of the model's entire lookback window — evidence the long-horizon trend feature really was doing real work, not just sitting there for show.

**Where Things Actually Stand**

None of this is a declaration that the Swiss franc is now a money machine. It is one pair, one particular slice of market history, one account's real cost structure rather than an idealized one, and a genuinely new kind of result for this project to sit with rather than immediately act on. But it cleared every bar this investigation has ever set for anything, including bars invented specifically because earlier promising-looking results kept failing to clear them. Gold proved this kind of search could occasionally work. The Swiss franc proved it could work somewhere our heroine is actually allowed to place a trade. That is meaningfully further than this whole line of inquiry has ever gotten before, and exactly the kind of thing a track with less traffic on it was supposed to produce, eventually, if it existed at all.
